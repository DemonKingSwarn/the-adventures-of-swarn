# Chapter 10: We Took a Shortcut Through Hell. Literally.

We left the car at Uttar Pradesh—damn, that thing is fast. I guess the god of travelers would have a fast car. We were inside Narad's Abode for a literal two days, and it felt like minutes to us.

Ria left a note on the car's windshield with the keys. The note said, "You can have it back now."

Now we needed to find the entrance to Patal more than ever.

"So how will we know we found the entrance?" I asked.

"Well, the entrance is probably where we will see a lot of souls forming a line to enter it," Ria replied. "Maya hides our mythical world from humans, so it could be right in front of our eyes, because sometimes Maya even works on us demigods too," she added.

I looked at Aryan, and he was already trying to flirt with some stranger.

"Don't you have a girlfriend or something?" I said.

To which Aryan just shrugged.

"So will we find Diti and Danu in Patal too?" I asked Ria.

"Uhm, I don't think so. They aren't dead, after all, plus they aren't the chthonic gods," Ria replied.

…

We continued walking till we found a building that read "Yama's Concert! Will start at 21:00."

"This must be the entrance," Ria said.

We looked at her and then proceeded to enter the building. And what we saw was thousands, or even more, dead souls waiting in some sort of waiting area while something let them pass one by one.

The air smelled like old incense and rain. Nobody was talking. Everyone just stood there, patient in the way only the dead can be, holding numbers like it was a bank queue.

There was this man who looked like he had been there since 1990, and then there was this woman who wore clothes from the 1700s.

We approached the man at the counter.

"I smell the living," he said. "Who are thee?"

"Hello, sir, uhm, we need to get to Patal," I said nervously.

"Wait for your turn, young one. You aren't dead yet," he said.

"But we need to—" Ria said, but then the man interrupted her.

"I smell the scent of demigods. I don't like them in my office," he said.

As he approached to grab us, we ran through his door, which led us straight to Patal without his permission.

There we were, in the realm of Patal, where all the dead souls come, but we weren't scared. Okay, we were a little scared.

We might have passed that receptionist's gate to enter here, but we still weren't in Patal. We needed to cross the river Vaitarani to truly enter it.

I tried to use the winged shoes Sujan gave me to cross the Vaitarani, but the river didn't like it and started pulling me. Except it didn't just pull me—it wanted me. I could feel it, the same way I could feel the water in the fountain back at the museum, except this water was cold and old and full of other people's regrets. Ria and Aryan managed to get hold of me just in time before I fell into the river.

"Phew! That was close," I said.

I crossed that idea off, and we walked forward towards the river, where we saw a ferryman taking souls across. And there were a few who tried to go across without his help and got stuck in the river eternally.

"That's Tarakeshwar—uhm, one of your dad's forms, who ferries the souls across," Ria said.

We approached the ferryman, and I moved forward.

"Uhm, hi, Dad?" I said nervously. "Are you actually, uhm, his form?"

Tarakeshwar just looked at me but didn't say anything.

"Uhm, we need to go across the river," I said, slowly.

Tarakeshwar just gestured for us to get into his boat.

Once we were in, he started rowing.

While we were in the boat, we saw that the waters of the river Vaitarani had turned black, and lots of toys, watches, and other possessions were floating in it.

"The incomplete human desires have been polluting these waters. The moment they die, they start bringing their incomplete desires with them here," Tarakeshwar said in a raspy voice. "The waters here used to be so clean that you could see the souls trapped in them from trying to cross it themselves. Now all you see is their guilt, their unfinished desires in the realm of the living."

None of us said anything for the rest of the ride. There was just the splash of the oar, and the black water, and the things floating in it that I tried very hard not to look at.

…

We reached the end of our journey. We got out of the boat and bowed to show our respect to Tarakeshwar as he turned his boat around and went back.

We were standing in the Pitriloka, the neutral zone for the souls who weren't truly evil or heroic—for the ones who lived an average life, in terms of their deeds, obviously. While the truly heroic beings go to Vaikuntha, the eternal paradise.

Picture this: a vast amount of trees, with souls living in some sort of cave-like structures and ash marks on the ground, and you would be able to picture the Pitriloka.

"I never actually considered that Patal had a neutral zone, or even an eternal paradise, before. I always thought it was only Narak," I said slowly, feeling a bit embarrassed by my knowledge of Patal.

"That's okay. Most people think the same," Ria replied softly.

That is when we encountered two four-eyed hounds. They were growling at us.

"Those are Yama's hounds, Sharvara and Shyama," Ria mumbled slowly.

"What are you two doing here?" I said. I don't know why, but I felt like I could talk to them.

They woofed at us—at least, I thought they did, because I could hear them speaking to me.

"You ask us the question we should be asking you?" Sharvara said.

Ria and Aryan looked at me with wide eyes, because they thought I had lost my mind.

"How am I able to understand you guys?" I asked, just as intrigued as Ria and Aryan were.

"It is because of your connection to your father. He is the lord of animals, boy," Shyama said. "Now get out of here, and come back when you are dead," he added.

"Please?" I asked, pleadingly.

That's when Ria threw a rubber ball into the distance, and both Shyama and Sharvara ran after it.

"Hurry, guys, they won't be distracted for long," Ria said urgently.

Part of me was sad to see them go. They were the friendliest creatures we had met on this entire quest.

So we ran towards the palace. We were here just to take the shortcut to Dandakaranya, and now it was kind of starting to feel like a really bad idea.

The palace was grand and made of black marble. Though surprisingly, the garden was lush green—for a realm that contains dead souls. Which I believe is probably because of Yama's twin sister Yami, or as most know her, Yamuna.

We reached the heart of the palace, where we found the big, magnificent throne, and by the side of it stood a man with a book in his hand.

We slowly approached the throne. Every step felt heavier than the last. The souls we passed didn't even look at us. They just kept sweeping ash off the ground, over and over, like it mattered. That's when Yama turned to face us from his throne and started walking down to us.

He wore a black suit that felt like it had thousands of souls trapped in it, and his skin was as pale as a vampire's. He had long, messy black hair.

And then we got a clear look at the man on the side. He was Chitragupt, and he wore the simple dress of a lawyer.

"So you finally found your way to me on your own accord," Yama said. "I was about to send them to you again. The other one wasn't this much of a problem like you are."

"What do you mean?" Ria asked.

"Well, let's just say I sent one of my Yamaduttas to retrieve this boy—after all, his father is also the one who broke the oath, just like Vishnu. And I would be called biased if I didn't come for him too, like I did for that girl," Yama said.

And at exactly that moment, a bat-like humanoid creature flew at us and stood at the backrest of Yama's throne. That's when it clicked that she was Mrs. Shristi, the substitute teacher who attacked me at the museum.

My hands curled into fists on their own. The last time I had seen her, she was turning to dust all over my shoes.

"Nothing personal, kid. It's just, they took the oath on my name, and I take these kinds of things very seriously," Yama said.

Then I looked at Ria, who, by the looks of it, was getting angry—maybe because it was because of Yama that Astrid had almost died before Vishnu turned her into a tree.

Her hand had drifted to her dagger.

And then I saw what was on the other side of the throne. It was my mom, in gold form, frozen. My breath hitched.

For a second, the whole palace fell away—the throne, Yama, Ria, Aryan, all of it. There was just her. Her eyes were open. Her face was calm, and I hated how calm she looked, frozen there like a trophy on a shelf.

I took a step towards her before I even knew I was moving. Ria caught my sleeve.

Every night since the border, I had replayed the golden dust on the grass. And here she was. Not dust. Not gone. Right there, close enough to touch, and I couldn't touch her.

"Oh, you noticed? I sent that Mahishasura to fetch your mom for me, as leverage to get you here," Yama said. "I can let her go, if you leave yourself to me."

"Will you let her go if we help find out what is wrong with Dandakaranya?" I asked quietly.

"Oh, a deal? Well, I have been known for making deals. I can accept that. But I wonder how you will escape my realm, hm? Even other gods have to obey me," Yama said. "Well, my armour was stolen from me. If you can fetch it, then sure, go ahead—I want to see you try," he added.

"We accept this deal," I said.

"Well then, let's see how you can get out of this realm without my permission, because no god is coming to help you here," Yama said.

But then I had an idea, and I closed my eyes and started praying to Diti. He said no god dared to interfere in the matters of his realm, but surely she could.

I pictured Diti's face—the way she had stroked my hair, the way she had said I would have her blessing. Behind me, Yama laughed, slow and unbothered, like a man who had never once lost.

I said in my mind, "Uhm, hello? It's me. Can you, uhm, get me and my friends out of Patal?"

I opened my eyes and hoped Diti had gotten my prayer.

Nothing happened. One heartbeat. Two. Yama started to smile, the way adults smile when they think a kid has run out of tricks.

And then, in a flash of light, we were out of Patal, lying on the forest floor.
