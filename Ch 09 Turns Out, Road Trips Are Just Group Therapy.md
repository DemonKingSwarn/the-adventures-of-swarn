# Chapter 9: Turns Out, Road Trips Are Just Group Therapy

I have never driven a car in my life. Turns out, when you steal the car of the god of travelers, it doesn't really matter.

The car practically drove itself. I barely had to touch the wheel, and it just knew where to go, gliding past trucks and buses like they were standing still. Outside the windows, the world blurred into streaks of yellow and green. Nobody said anything for a long time. I think we were all waiting for Narad to appear in the back seat and throw us into another one of his prisons.

He didn't.

"Okay," Aryan said finally, from the back seat. "I take it back. Stealing from a god is fun."

"You said 'mhm' back there," I said. "That was your whole review of being kidnapped into an illusion."

"I was processing."

We laughed. It felt wrong to laugh, with the prophecy and the quest and everything hanging over us, but we needed it.

Then we stopped at a toll booth, and the date on the receipt said we had lost two whole days inside Narad's Abode. Two days, gone, just like that. It had felt like minutes. Ria went quiet for a while after that, doing the math on how little time we had left, and Aryan kept saying "time is fake anyway," which didn't help anyone.

Around evening, Aryan's stomach growled so loudly that I heard it over the engine.

"Don't look at me like that," he said. "Bhutas get hungry too."

"We literally ate at Narad's—" I started, and then realised we hadn't. We had escaped before any of us got food. Ria checked the bag Vajivega had packed and found some mortal money, so when we spotted a roadside dhaba glowing in the dark like a little yellow island, we pulled over.

If you have never eaten at a highway dhaba at night, I don't know how to explain it to you. Plastic chairs, a TV playing some old movie nobody was watching, trucks rumbling past every few minutes, and the best food you will ever eat in your life. Aryan ordered enough for five people. Ria got chai. And for the first time since we left camp, the three of us just sat there, with no monsters, no gods, no illusions. Just three kids at a plastic table.

That's when it hit me. Out of nowhere. One minute I was tearing into a roti, and the next I couldn't breathe properly, because the last time I had eaten somewhere that wasn't camp, it was at the cabin, and my mom was stealing bites of her own cooking and booping my nose.

I put the roti down.

"Swarn?" Ria said.

"Sorry, I—" My voice came out wrong. "I keep thinking she's going to walk in and sit down with us. She loved places like this. Roadside stuff. She said fancy restaurants had no soul."

Neither of them said anything. They didn't try to fix it with some big wise speech. Aryan just quietly pushed his plate of pakoras towards me, which in Aryan language is basically a hug, and Ria rested her chin on her hand and looked at me the way she looks at a puzzle she actually cares about.

"I keep going over it," I said. "The border. Her taking my jacket. I keep thinking, what if I had run faster? What if I had uncapped the pen sooner? She'd be here. She'd be sitting right there, probably embarrassing me in front of you both."

"You fought Mahishasura with your bare hands," Ria said softly. "Twelve-year-old demigods don't do that. There was nothing more you could have done."

"She bought us time," Aryan said. He wasn't looking at either of us. He was looking at the road, at the trucks going past. "That's what protectors do. We buy time. Sometimes it's enough."

He said it quietly, and I knew he wasn't only talking about my mom.

"Aryan," I said. "Astrid wasn't your fault."

"It was my duty. I was supposed to get all three of them inside the border." He shrugged, but it was the fake kind of shrug. "Ria was seven. Sujan was ten. And Astrid stayed behind so I wouldn't have to choose. I think about that night a lot."

Ria reached over and put her hand on Aryan's arm. Nobody said anything for a while. The TV kept playing. A truck honked. It should have been awkward, but it wasn't. It was the opposite of awkward.

"For what it's worth," Ria said finally, "she would have hated you blaming yourself. She used to say guilt is just love with nowhere to go."

"She did not say that," Aryan said.

"No," Ria admitted. "But she would have, if she thought of it. She was very quotable."

And just like that we were laughing again, wet-eyed and tired, and some truck driver at the next table looked over at us like we were insane. Maybe we were.

We stayed there till the chai went cold and Aryan had eaten everything on the table, including, I am pretty sure, the little plate of sliced onions nobody else touched.

…

We drove through the night. Ria fell asleep in the back seat somewhere past midnight, curled up with her bag as a pillow. Aryan took the passenger seat and stayed awake with me, because Bhutas barely sleep, and because, I think, he didn't trust the car not to eat us.

"You've been quiet," he said, after a long stretch of just engine hum. "Quieter than usual."

I thought about lying. Then I thought about the prophecy, and figured there was no point.

"The last line of the prophecy," I said. "The betrayal one. I keep trying to figure out who it is. I look at everyone at camp differently now. Even you two, sometimes. And I hate it."

Aryan was quiet for a moment.

"For what it's worth," he said, "if I ever betray you, you have full permission to haunt me. Bhutas believe in ghosts, so that would be very scary for me."

I snorted. "You're ridiculous."

"I'm your protector. Protecting you includes your mood." He grinned, and then his face went serious again. "Whoever it is, Swarn, we'll see them coming. That's what the three of us do."

I wanted to believe him so badly.

In my bag, between my feet, the winged shoes Sujan had given me shifted. Just once. A tiny hum, like something turning over in its sleep. I told myself it was just the road. The car was a god's car, after all. Everything in it was probably a little alive.

But I moved my bag further from my feet anyway.

…

By the time the sky started turning grey, we were crossing into Uttar Pradesh. The car slowed down on its own, like it knew its job was done, and rolled to a stop on the side of an empty road.

Ria woke up, stretched, and looked at the horizon.

"We walk from here," she said. "The entrance to Patal has to be close."

I got out and looked back at the car. Somewhere behind us was camp, and Sujan, and the Shiva cabin, and the peepal tree. Somewhere ahead of us was the land of the dead, and, if Yama's Yamaduttas had told the truth, my mom.

"Hey," Aryan said, softly, reading my face like he always could. "We're getting her back."

"Yeah," I said. "We are."

And for the first time since the museum, I actually believed it.
