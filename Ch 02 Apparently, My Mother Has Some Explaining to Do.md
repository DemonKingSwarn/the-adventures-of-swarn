# Chapter 2: Apparently, My Mother Has Some Explaining to Do

And I am back, sitting in front of our principal. Even though I told everyone that I didn't touch Alison, they still got me here. And now I am getting expelled from yet another school.

Third school in three years. I could see the number floating in front of me the whole time the principal talked, like some kind of record I never wanted to break.

"So, what is your excuse for pushing Alison into that fountain?" the principal asked.

"I didn't touch Alison!" I replied.

…

I came out of his chambers, went to the nearby bench, and sat on it, thinking about everything I knew had happened. Then Mr Sharma came and said, "You know, you are special…"

I always loved his stories and all, but right now I didn't want to hear it, so I lashed out. "Look, Mr Sharma, you don't have to say it to cheer me up. I know I am in trouble."
I didn't even say goodbye to him and took the last bus to Bangalore.

I looked back once, from the bus window. He was still there by the bench, watching me go. He didn't look angry at me for lashing out. He looked worried. I didn't understand why until much later.

When the bus stopped for refreshments, I stumbled across Aryan, who hugged me really tight, as if he was scared.

That's when I saw two weird people. One of them was a beautiful woman and the other was a man. The woman was writing something in her book. But both of them had their eyes on me, like they were judging me.

Not the way strangers look at you when you're being loud. The way you look at something you have been searching for.

I looked at Aryan and asked, "Do you see them?"

"See who?" he replied.

It confused me. Like, how could he not see them? They were standing right there. And when I looked back at them, they were gone.

Aryan suddenly started shivering, like he always did whenever he wanted to use the toilet.

"Wait here, I am coming back from the toilet," he said.

As he went into the washroom, I didn't really wait for him. I got back onto the bus.

I know how that sounds. But right then, all I wanted was to go home—to my mom, to our tiny apartment, to normal problems.

…

I got home and entered the apartment, and the first thing I saw was my ugly, smelly step-dad and his equally piece-of-trash friends playing Teen Patti. They were so busy playing that they didn't even see me entering.

As I got out of my shoes, he noticed me.

"Oh, look who's back," he said in his nasty fat voice.

I didn't reply to him and tried to ignore him.

As I went to find my mom in her room, I noticed she was reading a book while sitting on the small balcony we have.

That balcony was my favourite place in the whole world. It was tiny—barely fit two chairs—but my mom had filled it with plants growing out of old paint buckets, and in the evenings she would sit there with her book and her chai, and for a while everything felt okay.

"Hey, Mom," I said, trying to be cheerful.

"I got the call from Pallavi High," she said.

I was really hoping they hadn't told her this.

"So what was it this time?" she asked.

She didn't shout. She never shouted. She just closed her book and looked at me with those tired eyes, and somehow that was worse than any shouting.

I told her everything that had happened at the museum. And her face went as pale as the moon.

I had expected her to be angry, or disappointed, or to not believe me at all. I had not expected fear.

"We need to go now," she said, urgently.

"But where?" I asked.

"I will explain everything to you there," she replied.

"Roshan! Come here," she said, calling to my step-dad.

"I wonder how anyone expects me to work," he said, coming over grumpily.

"We are leaving for the mountain cabin. We will be back during the weekend. Try not to burn the house down," she said.

"Since when do you go there?" he said.

"I am also taking your car," she replied.

My mom and I packed everything we needed, as usual, for our mountain cabin. It has always been our little escape. We have been going there for years now.

Except this time, my mom packed like the house was on fire. No list, no folding, just things thrown into bags. And she kept checking the window.

…

I fell asleep during the car ride, and then I remember I was standing somewhere on some kind of mountain with a hooded figure staring right at me.

The wind was freezing, the kind of cold that gets into your bones, and I couldn't move. He didn't say anything. He just watched me, like he was waiting for something. Under the hood, where his face should have been, there was nothing I could make out at all.

I woke up to my mom's voice calling me.

I looked around and realised we had arrived at the cabin we always visit during my vacations.

"So, wanna see who gets in first?" she asked.

And I burst out running towards the cabin.

For a second, with the cold evening air and the trees and my mom laughing behind me, it felt like every other trip. Like the museum had never happened.

Once we were inside, she asked me to freshen up. She would tell me everything after that.

I went to the washroom and freshened up.

I went back down and found her cooking. I went over and took a few bites of the chicken she was cooking.

She laughed and booped my nose.

"Mom!!!! Sto—op," I said and started laughing.

I want to remember that part exactly as it was. Her laughing. The smell of the chicken. The two of us just being us. Because of everything that happened after, this is the memory I hold on to.

And then, once we sat down on our couch, she looked at me like she was finding her words.

"So, a long time ago, I met a man… who was unlike any other man. And… only after you were born did I realise why he wasn't like anyone else. Because he wasn't a man. He was a god," she said, stuttering a little.

Her hands were trembling. I had never seen my mom's hands tremble before.

"What do you mean by a god? Like Jesus?" I replied, with a bit of suspicion.

"Not God, I mean a god," she said.

"Look, Mom, these are just stories. I know I am broken, but you don't have to cook up stories to say otherwise. And I know for a fact that there are no such things as gods, and there is definitely nothing like half-god," I retorted while standing up.

Her face crumpled at the word "broken," like I had hit her. That word had just slipped out of me. I didn't even know I believed it until I heard myself say it.

"Look, Swarn…" She was about to say something when we heard banging on our door.

When we opened the door, it was Aryan. He was panting. And then, for the first time, I looked at his legs.

He was talking to my mom, but I kept saying his name till he turned to me. "Aryan, why are your legs so scaly, like a lizard's?" I said, looking surprised.

He looked at me and then back at my mom. "You didn't tell him about me?" he said.

"You are early," she said.

"What is going on here? Can someone explain?" I said, frustrated.

"We will have to talk about the rest in the car," she said.

We got back into the car, and then I asked Aryan again, "Tell me why your legs are so weird."

"It's because I am a Bhuta," he said, calmly.

"A what?" I asked.

"I have been assigned as your protector, to bring you safely to camp," he said.

"My protector?" I said, mockingly, because it was always me who protected him from bullies. "And to what camp?" I asked again.

He looked again at my mom while she was driving and said, "You didn't tell him about camp?"

"I told you that you were early," she said.

He turned to me and said, "So, Camp Ashvattha is where kids like you are safe. That's where I was supposed to take you. But none of us saw Mrs. Shristi coming. Usually I can sense monsters from a mile away easily, but this time the Maya was strong, even for me."

"The what now?" I asked.

"Maya is what hides the mythical world from the humans. It makes them see what their minds can comprehend," he replied.

I was about to hit him with another question, but then my mom said, "Boys!"

"I am actually 27," Aryan said.

I looked through the rear window.

"What is that? And why is it wearing underwear?" I asked, frightened.

Aryan looked through the rear window and got scared too.

But my mom swerved between the trucks, hoping to avoid it. Then it appeared on the right side of the car, where the driver's seat was, and just tackled us, and we rolled off the side of the road into the forest.

The world spun. Glass shattered somewhere. The whole time we rolled, my mom's arms were around me, holding me against her, taking the hits for both of us. When we finally stopped, the car was on its side and my ears were ringing, and the first thing I heard was my mom asking if I was okay. Not her. Me.

We got out of the car through the rear glass, and then Aryan pointed at a giant tree on the cliff and said, "That's the camp border. We will be safe once we are inside." And we started running towards it.

The tree was so far away. That's the thing nobody tells you about running for your life—how far away everything is. My lungs were burning, my shoes kept slipping on the wet grass, and behind us I could hear that thing tearing the car apart like it was made of paper.

That's when that buffalo came really close to us, and my mom hugged me and told me she loved me.

She held my face in both her hands. Her hands were still trembling, but her eyes weren't. "I love you," she said again, slower this time, like she needed me to actually hear it.

"Mom, what are you doing? Won't we all be safe inside the border, according to him?" I asked her.

"She's human. She can't enter camp," Aryan replied.

The words didn't make sense to me. I understood every word, but they didn't make sense. My mom couldn't come? Then I wasn't going either. It was that simple.

And then she went to Aryan and asked him, "Swear to me you will protect him, my only son. Say it."

"I swear," Aryan said hesitantly.

And a loud thunder could be heard, but there was no lightning.

"It is after his scent, right?" she asked Aryan.

Aryan nodded.

"So his smell coming from both sides will confuse it," she said.

And then she took my jacket and went towards the buffalo.

I screamed at her to come back. Aryan grabbed my arm, and I fought him—I really fought him—but he was so much stronger than he looked.

I looked at the sight. She waved my jacket, and the buffalo started going after her, and the moment the buffalo stood on two legs and held her up in his hands, she looked back at me. Just once. She wasn't crying. She wasn't even scared. She looked at me the way she sometimes looked at me when she thought I was asleep, like she was memorizing my face. And then he crushed her into dust.

There was no sound. That's the part I remember most. The world just went silent. Golden dust drifted down onto the grass where my mom had been standing a second before, and I stood there staring at it, waiting for her to step back out of it—to laugh, to boop my nose, anything.

She didn't.

Something in me broke, and something else in me woke up. That's when I got so angry that something changed in me. I took out the pen Mr Sharma gave me and uncapped it, and it turned into a bronze trident. And I lunged forward.
The buffalo tackled me, and I fell down with the trident nowhere to be found near me, but I got up and climbed onto its back, and with my bare hands I tore out one of his horns and stabbed him with it. As he turned to dust, I fell down.

I lay there next to the dust of the thing that had killed my mom, staring up at the sky. I didn't feel like a hero. I didn't feel anything at all. Aryan was saying something to me, but it came through muffled, like he was underwater.

Then I remember walking towards the big tree, and from there I could see there were a few cabins on the other side of it. I started walking towards the big one and collapsed at its stairs. The last thing I remember was seeing the silhouette of a girl and something I can't tell what it was.

The girl said, "Is he the one? He must be the one."

That's when I heard the other voice. "Hush, Ria." And then he stood right in front of me and said, "Welcome to camp, Swarn. We've been expecting you." And then I fell unconscious.
